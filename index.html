About

I am finishing an M.S. in Artificial Intelligence Engineering – Information Security at Carnegie Mellon, and most of my work circles one question: how do you know what a model is actually doing?

That question has pulled me in three directions. In evaluation, I built an agent-evaluation platform at Bank of New York that scores an agent's full execution trace instead of only its final answer. In interpretability, I trained linear probes on transformer activations to find the layer where a model's reasoning about financial comparisons stops improving. In security, I poisoned a retrieval corpus to steer a language model's sentiment, then built the detector that catches it.

Before Pittsburgh I finished a B.E. in Computer Science at BITS Pilani, Dubai, and spent two internships shipping systems people used daily — a proposal-generation app on Cloud Run, and a triage pipeline that read handwritten medical referrals.

What I am working toward

Interpretability past linear probes — sparse features and circuits rather than single directions. Evaluation that survives multi-turn agents, where the failure is usually in the trajectory and not the last message. Adversarial robustness of retrieval, which is where most deployed AI now gets its facts. I play capture-the-flag security competitions and hackathons; the recognition below came out of those.

Sentiment steering against RAG

First author 2025–2026 IEEE ICC 2026

A retrieval-augmented system repeats what is in its corpus. So an attacker who can add a handful of documents can change what the model says about a person or a topic without ever touching the question being asked.

What I did

I built the black-box attack simulation and the detector that answers it. The attack poisons a slice of the corpus with sentiment-shifted passages about public officials; the defence inspects retrieved passages before they ever reach the generator, so it does not depend on which model is generating.

Approach

Passages are embedded with a MiniLM sentence encoder and retrieved through a FAISS index, then generated with Llama 3.1. Detection is two independent signals over the retrieved set: cosine similarity, which catches the near-duplicate phrasing that poisoning tends to introduce, and a z-score on the sentiment distribution, which catches passages whose polarity sits outside the corpus baseline. Either signal can flag a passage, which is what keeps recall high.

Explainable intrusion detection for IoT

Third of five authors 2024–2025 Computers and Electrical Engineering

An intrusion detection system that flags traffic without saying why cannot be acted on. A network administrator needs to know which features drove the call before shutting down a device.

What I did

I built and evaluated the deep classifiers and the SHAP-driven feature selection step. The useful finding was not the accuracy — it was that the explanations were good enough to act on: the fifteen features SHAP ranked highest were sufficient on their own.

Approach

One-dimensional CNN, LSTM and Bi-LSTM classifiers, trained across four benchmark datasets so the result would not be an artefact of one capture: NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, TON-IoT and X-IIoTID. SMOTE handles the class imbalance that makes rare attacks look like noise. SHAP then produces global and local explanations, and retraining on only the top fifteen features tests whether those explanations are faithful.

Built with Python, TensorFlow, Keras, SHAP, LIME, imbalanced-learn, pandas, NumPy

HOPE

Lead developerMulti-agent CBT platform

Cognitive behavioural therapy works and costs more than most people can pay. Most software answers to that are a single chatbot that forgets you between sessions.

I designed HOPE as an orchestrated team of agents rather than one prompt. A session coordinator decides which stage of a CBT session the conversation is in and hands off to specialists: a memory extractor that parses each message into thought patterns, therapy goals and session insights; a reply drafter grounded in a library of cognitive distortions and homework exercises; and a crisis detector that reads every inbound message for self-harm indicators before any reply is generated. Long-term context lives in a vector store, with a rolling summary keeping sessions coherent without paying for the whole transcript on every turn.

Research

Both DOIs resolve. Author order is as published.

Two peer-reviewed papers. The engineering behind them is described under selected work; what follows is the record and why each result matters.

  1. Alan Mohan, Shalaka S. Mahadik, Mithun Mukherjee, Pranav M. Pawar, Raja Muthalagu, Jaime Lloret

    Detecting Sentiment Steering Attacks on RAG-enabled Large Language Models

    ICC 2026 — IEEE International Conference on Communications pp. 1–6 May 2026 10.1109/ICC59461.2026.11588193

    Why it matters
    Retrieval is how most deployed language models get their facts, and a corpus is far easier to edit than a model. We showed that four hundredths of a percent of a corpus is enough leverage to steer sentiment on open-ended questions — an attack that needs no access to the model, the prompt or the query.
    My role
    First author. I ran the attack simulation and designed the two-signal detector.
    Strongest supported result
    The detector is model-agnostic: it works on retrieved passages, not on generations, so it transfers across generators and retrievers rather than being tuned to one stack.

  2. Sapna Sadhwani, Ameya Navare, Alan Mohan, Raja Muthalagu, Pranav M. Pawar

    IoT-based intrusion detection system using explainable multi-class deep learning approaches

    Computers and Electrical Engineering (Elsevier) vol. 123, art. 110256 April 2025 10.1016/j.compeleceng.2025.110256

    Why it matters
    Intrusion detection is where explainability stops being a nicety. An operator who cannot see which features triggered a detection cannot justify isolating a device, and a model that needs the full feature vector cannot run on the hardware IoT networks actually use.
    My role
    Third of five authors. I worked on the deep classifiers and the SHAP-based feature selection.
    Strongest supported result
    A fifteen-feature subset chosen by SHAP detected attacks including denial of service, ransomware and cross-site scripting above 92% accuracy on every one of the four datasets — evidence that the explanations were faithful, not decorative. The résumé also records this journal as Q1-ranked.
